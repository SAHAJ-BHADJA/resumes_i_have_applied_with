# Round 4 — Communication & Collaboration
### ~45 min · "Tell me about a time..." · **The round is literally named Collaboration. That's the whole hint.**

---

## Why this round is not a formality

Your notes say the final block is **"unfortunately the highest-signal one."** At a ~200-person Series B startup, this round decides whether you're pleasant to sit next to for two years. Voxel's own job posting tells you the rubric in plain English:

> <cite index="19-1">The most successful engineers at Voxel are **curious, humble, coachable, and eager to grow** alongside the team as we continue to scale.</cite>

> <cite index="21-1">wants to grow quickly by **pairing with senior engineers**, owning features, and working across the application lifecycle</cite>

Four adjectives and one verb. **Every story you tell should demonstrate at least one of them:**

| They want | The story that proves it |
|---|---|
| **Curious** | You investigated something nobody asked you to investigate |
| **Humble** | You were wrong and said so, or credited someone else |
| **Coachable** | You got hard feedback and *changed* |
| **Eager to grow** | You went after something outside your comfort zone |
| **Pairing / collaboration** | **Every story has another human in it whom you worked *with*** |

**The failure mode:** six stories where you're a lone hero who saw the problem, fixed the problem, and saved the day. That's a fail in a round named Collaboration. **Every story needs a named other person, and in at least two of them, they should be right and you wrong.**

---

## STAR — the format, tightened

| | | Time |
|---|---|---|
| **S**ituation | Context. Just enough. Who, where, what was at stake. | 15s |
| **T**ask | What *you* were responsible for. Not the team — you. | 10s |
| **A**ction | What you did. **60% of the story lives here.** Decisions, trade-offs, the conversation you had. | 60s |
| **R**esult | What happened. **With a number.** And what you learned. | 20s |

**Total: ~2 minutes.** Time yourself. Most people run 5 minutes and lose the room at 3.

**The three most common failures:**
1. **The Situation eats the story.** 90 seconds of context, 20 seconds of what you did. Cut the context to one sentence.
2. **"We" instead of "I."** They're evaluating *you*. Say "the team decided X, and I owned Y" — that's both honest and specific.
3. **No number in the Result.** "It went well" is not a result. "Cut the p99 from 800ms to 120ms" is. **If you have no number, you have no result.** Go find one before the 21st.

### Story 1 — Conflict / disagreement with a teammate ⭐ **the most important one**

**Covers:** "Tell me about a disagreement with a colleague" · "A time you had to convince someone" · "When you and your team disagreed on a technical approach" · "Someone whose work style clashed with yours"

**What they're checking:** Can you disagree without it becoming personal? Do you actually update on evidence? **The round is named Collaboration and this is the collaboration story.**

**Shape it like this:**
- A **technical** disagreement, not an interpersonal one. "He was lazy" is a story about you.
- Show you **understood their position** before arguing. Steelman it in the telling.
- **How you resolved it matters more than who won.** Data? A prototype? A time-boxed experiment? Escalation? A coin flip and a commitment?
- **Ideally, you were partly wrong.** Or you lost and committed anyway and it worked out. That's the version that gets you hired.

**The trap:** the story where you were right, they were wrong, you proved it, and you were vindicated. That story tells them exactly nothing about whether you can be disagreed *with*. **They already know you think you're right. They want to know what happens when you're not.**

**Your one-line prompt:**
> _______________________________________________
> *(a technical decision where a teammate disagreed with you, and the resolution wasn't just "I won")*

---

### Story 2 — Receiving hard feedback ⭐ **"coachable" is on their JD in writing**

**Covers:** "Tell me about difficult feedback you received" · "A time you were criticized" · "How do you handle feedback?" · "A weakness you've worked on"

**What they're checking:** the word "coachable" is not decoration. They said it. This round-2 pair-programming format exists partly to test it. **This story is the direct evidence.**

**Shape it like this:**
- The feedback should be **real and slightly uncomfortable to say out loud.** "I work too hard" is a non-answer and everybody knows it.
- **Your first reaction can be honest** — "I was defensive for about a day" is a *good* line. It's human, and it makes the change believable.
- **The change must be concrete and observable.** Not "I learned to communicate better." Rather: "I started posting a written plan in the PR description before writing code, and my reviewer stopped having to ask what I was doing."
- **Bonus: show it stuck.** "I still do that two years later."

**The trap:** a humblebrag disguised as feedback. If your "hard feedback" makes you look good, it isn't hard feedback, and the interviewer has heard the fake version forty times.

**Your one-line prompt:**
> _______________________________________________
> *(feedback that stung, and the specific thing you do differently now)*

---

### Story 3 — A project that failed or slipped

**Covers:** "A time you failed" · "A project that didn't go as planned" · "A missed deadline" · "A mistake you made" · "Something you'd do differently"

**What they're checking:** ownership without self-flagellation. Can you name your own contribution to a failure without either blaming others or performing guilt?

**Shape it like this:**
- **Own your specific part.** Not "requirements were unclear" — "I didn't push back on unclear requirements, and I should have on week one."
- **Name what you'd actually do differently.** Specific, mechanical, repeatable.
- **Show you did it differently next time.** That's the closing beat.
- Startups ship fast and break things. A candidate who has never shipped a failure has never shipped much.

**The trap:** blaming the org, the PM, the deadline, or the legacy code. Even when true, it's not the story they asked for. **They asked what *you* did.**

### Story 5 — Going beyond your role / ownership

**Covers:** "A time you took initiative" · "Went above and beyond" · "Owned something end-to-end" · "Saw a problem nobody assigned you"

**What they're checking:** their posting says engineers <cite index="19-1">own end-to-end product flows</cite>. Startups this size heavily screen for ownership. This is the "would you notice the thing that's broken and fix it, or file a ticket and move on" story.

**Shape it like this:**
- You **noticed** something nobody assigned you
- You **checked whether it mattered** before spending time on it (this is the part that separates ownership from thrash)
- You **did it** — and it stuck, someone still uses it
- **Bonus: you brought someone with you.** Collaboration is the theme.

**The trap:** the story where you went rogue and shipped something nobody wanted. Ownership without judgment is a liability, and the interviewer is listening for whether you know the difference.

### Story 6 — Debugging something painful ⭐ **your best technical-depth vehicle**

**Covers:** "The hardest bug you've fixed" · "A production incident" · "A time you had to learn something fast" · "When you were completely stuck"

**What they're checking:** methodology, not heroics. **This is the story where your Round 2 skills show up in narrative form.**

**Shape it like this:**
- **The method is the story.** Hypothesis → test → eliminate → narrow. Not "I stared at it and then had an idea."
- Say what you **ruled out** and how. That's the part that sounds like an engineer.
- The **"oh"** moment — what did the root cause turn out to be, and why was it hidden?
- **What you changed so it can't recur.** A test, a metric, an alert, a type. *"And I added a test that would have caught it"* is the closing line.
- **Who you asked for help.** Even here — especially here. A debugging story with no other humans in it, in a round named Collaboration, is a missed opportunity.

**The trap:** making it about how hard it was. Nobody cares that it took three days. They care about the *shape* of the three days.

# "Why Voxel"
### From your notes: *"the mission angle is easy and genuine here."* It is. Don't oversell it — just mean it.

## The 60-second version

> "Two things, one obvious and one less so.
>
> The obvious one: almost three million people in the US get injured at work every year, and the framing that got me is that these are *preventable* causes. Forklifts, spills, blocked exits. That's not an unsolved physics problem, it's a visibility problem — nobody was looking at that aisle at 2pm on a Tuesday. That's a genuinely rare case where the engineering problem and the reason to care are the same problem, and I don't think I've had that before.
>
> The less obvious one, and honestly the reason I'd pick Voxel over the other companies doing computer vision safety: **Actions.** Detection isn't the hard part anymore — a lot of companies can tell you a hazard happened. The hard part is that a dashboard full of red flags changes nothing. Voxel routes the detection to a specific person with a deadline and then measures whether it got fixed. That's a product decision that came from actually understanding that the customer isn't a data scientist, it's a site manager with forty other things to do.
>
> And selfishly: it's a small enough team that I'd be pairing with senior engineers and owning things end to end, on a stack that's Python and Go and real-time video at scale. That's the shape of the next two years I want."

## Why this works

- **Their own number** (~3 million) — you read their material
- **"Preventable"** — you understood the framing, not just the stat
- **Actions, specifically** — you looked past the homepage
- **A real differentiator** — detection vs resolution is the actual thesis of the company
- **Honest self-interest at the end** — nobody believes pure altruism, and the "selfishly" line makes the rest credible

## Trim it if they look bored

**The 15-second version:**
> "Three million preventable workplace injuries a year in the US, and the interesting part is that Voxel doesn't stop at detecting them — Actions routes it to a person with a deadline. Detection without resolution is just a dashboard. That's the part that made me apply."

## Two more you should have ready

**"Why this role / why now?"**
> "I want to be somewhere small enough that I own things end to end but big enough that there are people better than me to learn from. Two hundred people post-Series B is exactly that window. Your posting says pairing with senior engineers, which is the specific thing I'm optimizing for right now — I'd rather be the least experienced person on a strong team than the most experienced on an average one."

**"What do you know about what we do?"** ← *have this cold, they'll ask*
> "You use computer vision on the cameras a facility already has — which is the whole business model, since nobody's buying thirty new cameras — and detect leading indicators of risk: PPE, ergonomics, forklift proximity, spills, blocked exits. Then Actions turns that into assigned work, Executive Hub rolls it up for leadership, and Risk Insights, which I think launched at Modex in April, pushes a daily summary to site managers so they're not hunting through dashboards. Series B last June, and Ericsson came in strategically in October, which I'd guess is about the edge-compute angle since you're running inference on-site rather than streaming video to the cloud."

**That last clause — inferring *why* Ericsson invested — is the difference between reading a press release and understanding a company.**

# Questions To Ask Them
### **Not optional.** A candidate with no questions reads as a candidate who doesn't care.

**Ask 2–3 per round.** Pick ones the specific interviewer can actually answer — don't ask an IC about headcount strategy.

## For the engineers (Rounds 1, 2, 3)
- "What did you ship last sprint?"
- "What's the codebase like to work in? What's the part everyone's afraid of?"
- "How much of the stack does one person touch? Your posting says Go, TypeScript, Python — is that per-team or per-person?"
- "How do you handle the edge fleet? Deploying a model to thousands of appliances in buildings you don't own sounds genuinely hard."
- "What breaks most often?"
- "How does perception hand off to applications? Is that a clean API or is it messier than the org chart suggests?"
- "What's the review culture like? How long does a PR sit?"
- "What's something about working here that surprised you?"

## For the hiring manager
- "What does success look like at 30, 60, 90 days?"
- "What's the biggest engineering problem the team is facing right now that you'd hand to me?"
- "How's the team structured post-Series B? What's growing fastest?"
- "How do you think about the build-vs-buy line? You've got Timescale, Kafka, all of it — how do you decide?"
- "What separates someone who does well here from someone who struggles?"

## The good ones — that make them think
- **"What's the hardest part of the product that isn't the ML?"** *(Great question. The answer is almost certainly alert fatigue or edge fleet ops, and their answer tells you a lot.)*
- **"How do you measure whether an alert was useful?"** *(This is the actual product problem. Asking it says you understand the company.)*
- **"How do you handle the privacy conversation with customers? You're putting AI on cameras pointed at people at work — that has to come up in every deal."*
- **"Ericsson coming in strategically — is that pointing at edge compute? Is that changing the roadmap?"**
- **"What's a decision the team made that you disagreed with?"** *(Tests whether disagreement is safe here. Their answer tells you whether you want the job.)*

## Never ask
- ❌ Anything answerable from their website
- ❌ "What does the company do?"
- ❌ Comp or vacation in a technical round (that's the recruiter's conversation)
- ❌ "Do you have work-life balance?" (Ask *"what does a bad week look like here?"* — same information, and it gets a real answer instead of a defensive one)
